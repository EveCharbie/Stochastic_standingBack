\abstract{Optimal control theory has long been used to model acrobatic movements, producing kinematics that resemble athletes' motion. 
However, motor strategies generated through such deterministic optimal control neglect human motor variability and the perception-action coupling needed to execute complex real-life movements. 
Thus, they are only optimal from a motor perspective and fail to reproduce the sensorimotor strategies exhibited by athletes.
Indeed, since athletes strongly rely on their senses to achieve high performance, their need for accurate sensory input often impacts their  kinematics. To overcome these limitations, optimal feedback control theory can be used to incorporate motor noise, state variability, and feedback loops  subject to sensory noise. 
This study proposes an advanced stochastic optimal control framework that includes state-dependent sensory noise (such as vestibular acuity varying with head velocity) and prospective adaptations to the motor plan (such as moment of inertia modulations based on current somersault angular velocity). 
Using a full-body, torque-driven skeletal model, we generated motor policies for a backward somersault minimizing landing variability and joint efforts. 
The techniques generated through our stochastic implementation were compared with deterministic optimal control. 
The stochastic implementation successfully replicated sensory acquisition strategies used by gymnasts: reducing head rotational velocity to increase vestibular acuity and fixating the gaze on the floor to prepare for landing, behaviors that the deterministic implementation failed to replicate. 
Our stochastic optimal control framework opens the possibility to analyze the interaction between the sensory and motor strategies during complex human movements.}

\keywords{Predictive simulation, Somersault, Robust techniques, Visual feedback, Vestibular feedback}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% Human movement synthesis is important for what if scenarios
Predictive simulations, based on optimal control theory, have been used for decades by sports biomechanists wishing to test hypothetical cases (\textit{i.e.}, \textit{what-if} scenarios).
It is a quick, cheap, safe, and powerful tool allowing to predict how human move or should move to maximize/minimize performance criteria.
To improve the reliability of the movements synthesized through predictive simulations, efforts have been dedicated to creating more physiological human models and crafting cost functions generating movements matching more closely experimental kinematics \cite{febrer2023predictive}.
It helped synthesize realistic human movements for simple tasks like walking \cite{ezati2019review}.
However, for more complex tasks, motor strategies arising from optimal control are sometimes not suitable because they are not robust to motor variability.  
For example, Hiley \& Yeadon \cite{hiley2016investigating} showed that the technique used by athletes to perform giant circles before a double layout somersault dismount on high bars matched more closely the predictive simulations when maximizing success (\textit{i.e.}, robust technique) rather than minimizing joint torques.
Thus, to synthesize realistic movements, predictive simulations should consider human movement variability to generate techniques that are less affected by execution variations.

One way humans deal with motor variability is through motor corrections based on sensory information.
Leveraging optimal feedback control theory \cite{todorov2004optimality}, these motor corrections have been recently included into biomechanical predictive simulations by modeling motor noises and noisy sensory feedbacks in a stochastic optimal control approach \cite{van2022approximate}. 
It helped explain more complex motor control phenomenon like how muscle co-contraction can help dealing with uncertainty \cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}.
However, even with these improvements, current stochastic predictive simulations still falls short of modelling sensory acquisition strategies because they consider that sensory noise is constant throughout the movement.
Although sensory noise is hard to measure experimentally, there is still evidence that our senses sensitivity depends on the state of our body: hearing acuity decreases as the distance between the ear and the sound source increases, vestibular \cite{mallery2010human} and visual \cite{demer1993dynamic} acuity decreases as the head velocity increases, spatial orientation acuity decreases as the angle between the body and vertical increases \cite{kaptein2004interpretation}, etc. 
% (from 2.26$^\circ/s$ at 20$^\circ/s$ head rotational velocity to 5.16$^\circ/s$ at 150$^\circ/s$ head rotational velocity \cite{mallery2010human} and visual acuity 
Thus, some human movements exhibit sensory acquisition behavior aiming to increase sensory acuity, allowing for more appropriate motor corrections.
The backward somersault (acrobatic movement where a rotation is completed around the mediolateral axis) is an example of movement where athletes exhibit sensorimotor strategies: \textit{i)} shortly after take-off, they slow down their head rotation velocity, increasing visual and vestibular acuity (\textit{i.e.}, \textit{spotting}) \cite{davlin2004gymnasts}, and \textit{ii)} before landing, they reorient their head and eyes to look at the ground to make prospective corrections, leading to a balanced landing \cite{bardy1998body}.
These sensory acquisition strategies are observable through the kinematics showing that there is a perception-action coupling in the acrobatic technique. 
Therefore, to synthesize relevant sensorimotor strategies for complex tasks, predictive simulations should consider state-dependant sensory noise.
Moreover, studying complex movements such as the backward somersault where acute coordination and precise treatment of the sensory information is needed to succeed might help increase our understanding of the integration of sensory information into human motor control which is subtle and complex to measure.

The sensory information can be used to overcome unexpected internal or external disturbances by making corrections to the original motor plan (open-loop control).
This information can be used either directly (direct sensory feedback control) by comparing it with a predefined sensory reference to generate an error signal \cite{ghez2000organization} or in anticipation (anticipatory sensory feedback control) by using it to estimate the future state of the body through an internal forward model \cite{desmurget2000forward} and comparing this prediction with the desired state to generate an error signal.
Both error signals can be modulated and/or filtered before being combined with the original motor plan \cite{desmurget2000forward}.
Both direct and anticipatory feedback control are thought to be used by athletes during the backward somersault. 
Proprioceptive information would be used to estimate the current athlete's state, allowing for direct feedback control.
Visual information would be used to estimate the remaining time before ground contact \cite{bardy1998body} and vestibular information would be used to estimate the athlete's rotational velocity.
Combining the two information would allowing for anticipatory sensory feedback control.
These two types of feedback help athletes land with more balance through inertia modifications increasing/decreasing the somersault rotation velocity.
To the authors knowledge, only open-loop control and direct sensory feedback control has been considered in predictive simulations of human movements \cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}, leaving aside anticipatory sensory feedback control.
Since there is evidence that anticipatory sensory feedback control is used by athletes during the backward somersault, it will be introduced in the predictive simulation presented in this study.

% However, during rapid movements with large muscle contractions such as the backward somersault the amplitude modulation of the motor noise might influence the sensorimotor control strategy choices.

Although a few simulation studies investigated the backward somersault in humans \cite{khosravi1987control, leboeuf2012optimal, pettersson2013optimisation, farr2025including}, none of them considered sensory feedback or motor variability.  
Khosravi & al. \cite{khosravi1987control} used feedback torques to find the control strategy that best fits an athlete's technique.
However, the feedback torques were used as a computational trick to find an optimal open-loop control strategy and do not represent a sensory feedback.

% Park & Park \cite{park2012backflips} used sensory feedbacks to stabilize the robot's motion.
On the one hand, some studies \cite{khosravi1987control, leboeuf2012optimal} informed the optimization process with athletes' kinematics.
In this case, it is not surprising that the optimal solutions matched athletes' kinematics, but these studies do not provide insights into why athletes use these acrobatic techniques.
On the other hand, predictive simulations \cite{pettersson2013optimisation, farr2025including} generated backward somersault techniques only from the task requirements (without providing athletes techniques).
In this case, the optimal techniques resembled athletes' technique, but the spotting and floor fixation did not appear in the optimal strategies as no sensory feedback was not modeled.
To better match, and thus better understand, athletes behavior, further predictive simulations of the backward somersault are needed to include sensory feedback and motor variability.


The primary aim of this study was to generate an optimal sensorimotor strategy for the backward somersault.
An avatar subject to a nonlinear dynamics corrupted by motor noise was considered. 
To cope with motor noise, the avatar could use noisy vestibular, visual and proprioceptive information to make direct and anticipatory corrections.
An optimal acrobatic strategy was obtained by solving a discretized version of a stochastic optimal control problem.
The optimal techniques generated through our stochastic optimal control implementations were compared with the one generated through deterministic optimal control.
The stochastic implementations enabled the emergence of the two sensory acquisition strategies used by athletes, confirming its relevance for the generation of sensorimotor strategies of complex movements.

\subsection{Athlete avatar}
The athlete avatar was composed of 5 segments (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}): trunk, head, arms, upper-legs, and lower-legs.
Its inertial characteristics were set to match those of a national male trampolinist.  % A verifier que c'est vraiment Emile

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/model.png}
\caption{The five-segments athlete avatar used for the predictive simulations. It is composed of three unactuated degrees of freedom (blue): forward translation, upward translations, and somersault rotation and five actuated degrees of freedom (purple): ankle, knee, hip, shoulder, neck and eye rotations. Note that the eye rotation was only used in the SOCPA and SOCP+ simulations.}
\label{fig:model}
\end{figure}

The athlete avatar's generalized coordinates ($q$) were composed of unactuated and actuated degrees of freedom (DoFs).
The ankle, knee, hip, shoulder and neck joints were dampened by joint friction $- 0.1 \dot{q}$.
No torque was applied on each of the unactuated DoFs.
Whereas the torque applied on each actuated DoF ($\tau_{\text{total}}$) was composed of different components depending on the implementation considered (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:OCP}-\ref{subsec:SOCP_plus}).
The avatar was torque actuated and governed by the non-linear multibody dynamics equation:

\begin{equation}
F : \ddot{q} = M(q)^{-1} (\tau_{\text{total}} - N(q, \dot{q}) - v\dot{q}_{\text{joints}}) \label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q$, $\dot{q}$ and $\ddot{q}$ are the generalized joint coordinates, velocities and accelerations, respectively. $\tau_{\text{total}}$ are the torques applied on each joint, $M$ is the mass matrix, $N$ is the non-linear effect vector comprising Coriolis, centrifugal, and gravity effects, and $v$ are the friction coefficient set to $0.1~sNm$ for the ankle, knee, hip, shoulder and neck joints and to $0~sNm$ for the unactuated DoFs and the eyes.


\subsection{Discretized stochastic optimal control framework}
To transcribe the stochastic optimal control problems into a solvable deterministic optimal control problem, the motor and sensory noises were sampled from a Gaussian distribution at 20 different instances as in \cite{koelewijn2020solution}.
Analogically, it could be seen as if 20 optimal control problems were solved simultaneously with different noise values, and thus different state values, but with the same motor command.
The number of noise samples (20) was chosen based on the analysis in \cite{koelewijn2020solution} showing that the cost function value varied less than 1\% when using 20 or more samples.
The constant part of the motor and sensory noises ($w_{\circ}$) were sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a standard deviation of $5$ for the joint torque motor noise, $0.0005$ for the joint angle proprioception, $0.0045$ for the joint velocity proprioception inputs, $0.0005$ for the vestibular input, and $0.0005$ for the visual inputs. 

The optimal policies were computed using a composite cost function, where the main objective was to minimize landing condition variability.
There were other terms to minimizing the expected efforts and the expected efforts rate of change.
Finally, regularization terms were added to encourage a realistic motion: minimizing the duration of the movement, the direct feedback gains, and the direct feedback gains rate of change.

The optimization problems were implemented in Bioptim \cite{michaud2022bioptim} using the automatic differentiation provided by CasADi \cite{Andersson2019}, and solved using IPOPT \cite{wachter2006implementation} and the linear solver MA97 \cite{hogg2011hsl_ma97} with a tolerance of \num{1e-6}.
Each stochastic optimal control problem was warm-started with the optimal generalized coordinates, velocities and torques from the deterministic implementation.

Five different implementations of the problem were solved to generate optimal techniques for the aerial phase of a backward somersault:
\begin{itemize}
	\item[] \textbf{\textcolor{OCP_color}{OCP :}} A deterministic optimal control problem
	\item[] \textbf{\textcolor{SOCP_color}{SOCP :}} A traditional stochastic optimal control problem
	\item[] \textbf{\textcolor{SOCP_VARIABLE_color}{SOCPV :}} A stochastic optimal control problem with the addition of state-dependent sensory noise and signal-dependent motor noise
	\item[] \textbf{\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{SOCPA :}} A stochastic optimal control problem with the addition of anticipatory feedback control
	\item[] \textbf{\textcolor{SOCP_plus_color}{SOCP+ :}} A stochastic optimal control problem with the addition of both state-dependent sensory noise and signal-dependent motor noise and anticipatory feedback control
\end{itemize}

Each of the optimal control problem implementations are detailed in the following sections.
To simplify interpretation, each optimization variable is presented colored.

\subsubsection{Stochastic optimal control problem (\textcolor{SOCP_color}{SOCP})}\label{subsec:SOCP}
This basic stochastic implementation was composed of multiple episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:socp}). 
For each episode, motor and sensory noises were directly sampled from a Gaussian distribution.
The torques applied to the joints were composed of the voluntary motor command ($\textcolor{SOCP_color}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}$), direct feedback corrections ($\tau_{\text{dfb}}$), and motor noise ($\tau_{\text{noise}}$).
The direct feedback corrections were based on proprioceptive (joint positions and velocities) and vestibular (body spatial orientation) information. 
The direct feedback corrections applied to the joint torques ($\tau_{\text{dfb}}$) were a linear combination (feedback gain matrix: $\textcolor{SOCP_color}{K_{\text{dfb}}}$) of the differences between the current noised sensory information and a sensory reference ($\textcolor{SOCP_color}{\text{ref}_{\text{dfb}}}$).
The sensory reference was constrained to represent the mean sensory information across all episodes.
The sensory information was corrupted by random noise ($w_{\text{proprioception}}$ and $w_{\text{vestibular}}$).

\subsection{Optimization problem implementation}
\setcounter{equation}{26}
The optimization problems were transcribed using direct multiple-shooting.
The total duration of the movement ($T$) was included to the optimal control problem as an optimization variable.
The movement time was discretized into 16 time intervals of equal duration.
Continuity constraints ($g_{DMS}$) were imposed at each time interval transitions to ensure the joint generalized coordinates and velocities are continuous throughout the entire movement.
%
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{DMS}} : 
\begin{bmatrix}
q_{j+1} \\ 
\dot{q}_{i+1}
\end{bmatrix} 
= \int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}{
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{q}_i \\ 
F(q_i, \dot{q}_i, \tau_{{\text{total}}_i})
\end{bmatrix}
dt}
\end{equation}

Specific constraints were also added to ensure the athlete avatar landed the backward somersault on the floor ($g_1$) with its center of mass over it's feet ($g_2$).
%
\begin{eqnarray}
g_1 : P(q, T)_z = 0 \\
g_2: P(q, T)_y - CoM(q, T)_y = 0
\end{eqnarray}
%
\noindent where $P$ is the position of the forefoot marker and $CoM$ is the position of the center of mass.
An initial standing posture with extended body and arms upward, vertical velocity ($2~m/s$) and somersault velocity ($450\degree/s$) were imposed to match real athletes' take-off position and to make sure implementations were comparable.
For the stochastic version, an additional constraint was added so that the reference ($ref$) sensory input was equal to the mean sensory input ($S$) over all episodes.
\begin{equation}
g_3: ref(t_i) - \overline{S}(q, \dot{q}, t_i) = 0
\end{equation} 


The cost function was composed of multiple terms to generate the best acrobatic movement, while ensuring numerical stability.
The main objective of the stochastic optimal control problems was to minimize landing instability, which is defined as minimizing the variability in center of mass position, center of mass velocity and body angular velocity at landing.
\begin{eqnarray}
\phi_1: \num{1e+5}~\sum_{n}^{episodes}{(CoM(q_n, t_i)_y -\overline{CoM(q, t_i)})^2} \\
\phi_2: \num{1e+5}~\sum_{n}^{episodes}{(\dot{CoM}(q_n, t_i)_y -\overline{\dot{CoM}(q, t_i)})^2} \\
\phi_3: \num{1e+5}~\sum_{n}^{episodes}{(L(q_n, \dot{q}_n, t_i)/I(q_n, t_i) -\overline{L(q, \dot{q}, t_i)/I(q, t_i)})^2}
\end{eqnarray}
\noindent where $n$ is the number of the episode, $L$ is the whole body angular momentum, and $I$ is the whole body inertia.
An additional term was added to minimize the expected efforts needed to complete the acrobatic.
\begin{equation}
\phi_4 = 0.01~\int_{0}^{T}{\tau_{\text{total}}^2~dt}
\end{equation}
Another term minimized the expected efforts rate of change to promote a psychophysiological motor command.
\begin{equation}
\phi_5 = 0.01~\int_{0}^{T}{{\delta~\tau_{\text{total}}}^2~dt}
\end{equation}
Regularization terms were also added to ensure the feedback gains, feedback gains rate of change, and total movement duration stayed within acceptable rages.
\begin{eqnarray}
\phi_6 = \num{1e-5}~\int_{0}^{T}{K^2~dt} \\
\phi_7 = 1~\int_{0}^{T}{\delta ~K^2~dt} \\
\phi_8 = 0.01~T
\end{eqnarray}
The weights of the objective terms were manually fine tuned.


The complete approximate stochastic optimal control transcription was as follows:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll@{}ll}
\underset{q, \dot{q}, \tau_{\text{voluntary}}, \text{ref}, K, T}{\min} & \sum{\phi_i} \\
\text{~~~~~~~~~s.t.} & g_{\text{DMS}} \\
            & g_1 = 0 \\
            & g_2 = 0 \\
            & g_3 = 0 \\
            & q \in \mathcal{Q} \\
            & \dot{q} \in \dot{\mathcal{Q}} \\
            & \tau_{\text{voluntary}} \in \mathcal{\tau} \\
            & \text{ref} \in \mathcal{R} \\
            & K \in \mathcal{K} \\
            & T \in \mathcal{T} \\
\end{array}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Comparison of the optimal solutions}
The difference between the optimal kinematics from the different transcriptions of the problem were compared using statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D) \cite{pataky2012one}.
The optimal joint torques were split into their components to analyze the contribution of open-loop control (\tau_{\text{voluntary}}), joint damping, direct feedback control (\tau_{\text{dfb}}), and anticipatory feedback control (\tau_{\text{afb}}).
The head rotation velocity and eye orientation were analyzed to extract sensory acquisition strategies from the optimal acrobatic techniques.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Optimal kinematics}
An optimal solution (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinograms}) was reached for each formulation (\textcolor{OCP_color}{OCP}, \textcolor{SOCP_color}{SOCP}, \textcolor{SOCP_VARIABLE_color}{SOCPV} \textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{SOCPA}, \textcolor{SOCP_plus_color}{SOCP+}).
The duration of the acrobatic was similar across implementations (OCP: 0.405~s, SOCP: 0.403~s, SOCPV: 0.405~s, SOCPA: 0.404~s, SOCP+: 0.403~s). 
The formulation had an influenced the optimal kinematics found.
Indeed, statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D~\ref{pataky2012one}) highlighted a significant difference between optimal kinematics generated with different formulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics}).
All joint angles timeseries were significantly different between implementations for at leaat 53\% of the movement.
Notably, the knees were kept extented longer and the initial hip flexion was less pronounced in the deterministic version (OCP).

\subsection{Optimal motor strategies}

The motor command differed between the different implementation (Fig.~\ref{fig:torques_contributions}~a)).
The total torque actuation (integral of the total torque) was larger in the deterministic (OCP: -80.11~Nm.s) than in the stochastic (SOCP: -3.51~Nm.s, SOCPV:1.80~ Nm.s, SOCPA: 1.70~Nm.s, SOCP+: -2.22~Nm.s) transcriptions.
The largest torque contribution came from the for open-loop (98.97\%) for the OCP implementation, from direct feedback (65.15\%) for the SOCP implementation, from direct feedback (58.78\%) for the SOCPV implementation, from open-loop (38.80\%) and anticipatory feedback (38.38\%) for the SOCPA implementation, and from open-loop (39.51\%) and anticipatory feedback (35.75\%) for the SOCP+ implementation (Fig.~\ref{fig:torques_contributions}~b)).

\subsection{Optimal sensory acquisition strategies}

For all implementations, the feedback gains decreased shortly before landing (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensory_strategies} a)).
The hip feedback gains were maximal when fully tucked and fully extended during the middle part of the acrobatics.
When anticipatory feedback was available, its gain on the knees DoF reached the maximal bound (saturated) for the majority of the acrobatics.
The mean absolute eye orientation was smaller in the SOCP+ (77.56\degree) than in the SOCPV (80.34\degree), especially at the end of the acrobatics, when visual anticipatory feedback is available.

\subsection{Landing variability performance}

The main performance objective of the task considered in this study was to minimize the landing variability, which was measured as the sum of the variability of the CoM horizontal position, CoM horizontal velocity, and body angular velocity at the end of the movement.
The SOCP+ formulation allowed to reach the smallest landing variability (OCP: 20.061, SOCP: 1.178, SOCPV: 0.062, SOCPA: 0.034,
SOCP+: 0.030).
