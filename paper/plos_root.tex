\section*{Abstract}
Deterministic optimal control has long been used to model acrobatic movements, producing kinematics that partly reproduce athletes' motion. 
However, motor strategies generated by such a deterministic paradigm neglect human motor variability and the perception-action coupling required to execute complex real-life movements.
The resulting techniques are only optimal from a motor perspective and fail to reproduce the sensorimotor strategies exhibited by athletes.
Since athletes rely heavily on their senses to achieve consistent high performance, their need for accurate sensory input often affects their kinematics. 
To overcome these limitations, optimal feedback control theory can be used to incorporate motor noise, state variability, and feedback loops subject to sensory noise. 
This study proposes an advanced stochastic optimal control framework that includes state-dependent sensory noise (\textit{e.g.}, vestibular acuity varying with head velocity) and prospective adaptations to the motor plan (\textit{e.g.}, moment of inertia modulations based on current somersault angular velocity). 
Using a full-body, torque-driven skeletal model, we generated motor policies for a backward somersault that minimized landing variability and joint effort. 
The techniques generated by our stochastic implementation were compared with those of deterministic optimal control. 
The advanced stochastic implementation successfully replicated sensory acquisition strategies used by gymnasts: reducing head rotational velocity to increase vestibular acuity and fixating the gaze on the floor to prepare for landing.
The deterministic implementation failed to replicate theses strategies.
Our stochastic optimal control framework opens the possibility of analyzing the interaction between sensory and motor strategies during complex human movements.

\section*{Author summary}
Understanding how humans perform complex movements is a central question in fields like sports science, rehabilitation, and human-robot interactions. 
While computer simulations have helped advance our knowledge, the realism of generated movements decreases as movement complexity increases. 
We hypothesized that this limitation could be addressed by considering a detailed human model and more complex perception-action mechanisms involved in the generation of robust movements.
In this study, we introduced an innovative framework that better replicates human movements by considering that 1) human sensory capacities depend on its current state, and 2) human adapt their movements in anticipation of expected future events.
We tested this framework on a backflip, while considering that 1) vestibular capacities decrease with increasing head velocity, and 2) athletes modify their movements to achieve a successful landing.
Unlike earlier frameworks, ours replicates key sensorimotor strategies used by gymnasts: reducing head rotation velocity to facilitate spatial orientation and looking at the floor to prepare for landing.
These findings demonstrate that intertwining sensory acquisition and movement generation yields sensorimotor strategies that enhance movement precision. 
Beyond gymnastics, this research advances our understanding of motor control by highlighting how sensory information guides precise yet adaptable movements.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% Human movement synthesis is important for what if scenarios
Deterministic predictive simulations have been used by sports biomechanists for decades to test hypothetical cases (\textit{i.e.}, \textit{what-if} scenarios).
They offer an inexpensive, safe, and powerful means of predicting or optimizing human movements.
To improve the reliability of movements generated by predictive simulations, efforts have been dedicated to developing more physiologically realistic human models and crafting cost functions that more closely reproduce experimental kinematics (see \cite{febrer2023predictive} for a review).
Realistic human movements were generated for daily living tasks, such as walking \cite{ezati2019review}.
% \comEC{Ajouter l'article de Najoua lorsque dispo}
For sporting tasks, however, the agreement between techniques generated through deterministic optimal control and experimental data is sometimes less convincing.
This disagreement likely comes from maximizing only performance criterion during the optimization rather than maximizing for performance consistency.  
% as the optimal techniques lack the motor-variability regulation strategies exhibited by humans.  
For example, Hiley \& Yeadon \cite{hiley2016investigating} used a planar four-segment model to show that the technique used by athletes to perform giant circles before a double layout somersault dismount on high bars \hl{matched the predictive simulations more closely} when maximizing task success in the presence of uncertainty (\textit{i.e.}, robust technique) rather than minimizing joint torques.
Thus, to generate realistic movements, predictive simulations should account for the noisy nature of motor commands, ensuring that simulated techniques are less sensitive to execution errors.


One way humans cope with neuromuscular noise is by relying on sensory feedback to make online motor corrections.
Leveraging optimal feedback control theory, these motor corrections have been incorporated into biomechanical predictive simulations by modelling motor noise and noisy sensory feedback \cite{todorov2004optimality}. 
More recently, this stochastic optimal control framework was extended to complex musculoskeletal models with non-linear dynamics \cite{van2022approximate}. 
This approach helped explain more complex motor control phenomena, such as how muscle co-contraction can be energy-efficient by helping cope with uncertainty \cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}.
However, even with these advances, current biomechanical stochastic predictive simulations still fall short of modelling sensory-acquisition strategies because \hl{they simplify the modelling of either the relationship between the states and sensory noise \mbox{\cite{van2022approximate}} or the system's dynamics \mbox{\cite{todorov2005stochastic}}}.


Although sensory noise is difficult to measure experimentally, there is still evidence that sensory sensitivity depends on the state of the body \cite{mallery2010human, demer1993dynamic, kaptein2004interpretation}.
% : hearing acuity decreases as the distance between the ear and the sound source increases, vestibular \cite{mallery2010human} and visual \cite{demer1993dynamic} acuity decreases as the head velocity increases, spatial orientation acuity decreases as the angle between the body and vertical increases \cite{kaptein2004interpretation}, etc. 
% (from 2.26$^\circ/s$ at 20$^\circ/s$ head rotational velocity to 5.16$^\circ/s$ at 150$^\circ/s$ head rotational velocity \cite{mallery2010human} and visual acuity 
As a result, some human movements feature sensory-acquisition strategies aiming to increase sensory acuity, enabling more effective motor corrections \cite{yeo2016optimal}.
For instance, during the backward somersault, athletes use two sensorimotor strategies to achieve a consistent landing \cite{bardy1998body}: \textit{i)} shortly after take-off, they reduce head rotational velocity to increase visual and vestibular acuity (\textit{i.e.}, \textit{spotting}) \cite{davlin2004gymnasts}, and \textit{ii)} before landing, they orient their head and eyes to look at the ground to make prospective corrections.
These strategies aiming to increase sensory acuity, are observable in athletes' kinematics, demonstrating the tight perception-action coupling that underlies skilled acrobatics.
Therefore, to generate relevant sensorimotor techniques for complex tasks, predictive simulations should consider state-dependent sensory noise.
Moreover, studying complex movements (where precise coordination and accurate processing of the sensory information are required) may improve our understanding of how sensory information is integrated into human motor control.
Predictive simulation is an appropriate means to study sensorimotor integration, as this cognitive process is challenging to measure experimentally without making the task dangerous to realize.


Sensory information can be used to overcome unexpected internal or external disturbances by making corrections to the original motor plan.
This information can be used directly (direct sensory feedback control) by comparing it with a predefined sensory reference to generate an error signal \cite{ghez2000organization}. 
It can also be used in anticipation (anticipatory sensory feedback control) by using it to estimate the future state of the body through an internal forward model \cite{desmurget2000forward} and compare this prediction with the desired state to generate an error signal.
Both error signals can be modulated, weighted and/or filtered before being combined with the original motor plan \cite{desmurget2000forward}.
During the backward somersault, both direct and anticipatory feedback control might be used by athletes \cite{bardy1998body}. 
Proprioceptive information would be used by athletes to estimate their current joint state, allowing for direct feedback control.
Visual information would be used to estimate the remaining time before ground contact \cite{bardy1998body}, and vestibular information would be used to estimate the athlete's somersault velocity.
Combining these two pieces of information with proprioceptive information would enable anticipatory sensory feedback control through a forward model predicting the somersault angle at landing.
These two types of feedback help athletes land with more balance by modifying inertia to adjust the somersault rotation velocity.
\hl{Stochastic optimal control simulations generally rely only on open-loop control and direct sensory feedback control to actuate the skeletal model \mbox{\cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}}, leaving aside anticipatory sensory feedback control.}
Given evidence that athletes use anticipatory sensory feedback control during backward somersaults, predictive simulations must account for such control mechanisms.

% Another shortcoming of the current implementations of stochastic optimal control is that they consider constant motor noise.
% However, it was shown that motor noise is signal-dependent \cite{jones2002sources}.
% Considering constant motor noise might have a negligible impact on the optimal sensorimotor strategies generated for slow reaching movements or balance control previously studied \cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}.
% However, during rapid movements with large muscle contractions, such as the backward somersault, the amplitude modulation of the motor noise might influence the sensorimotor control strategy choices.

Although a few simulation studies investigated the backward somersault in humans \cite{khosravi1987control, leboeuf2012optimal, pettersson2013optimisation, farr2025including}, none of them considered sensory feedback or motor variability.  
Khosravi \& al. \cite{khosravi1987control} used feedback torques to find the control strategy that best fits an athlete's technique.
However, the feedback torques were used only as a computational trick and in the absence of noise, this feedback control strategy is equivalent to open-loop control. 
% Park & Park \cite{park2012backflips} used sensory feedbacks to stabilize the robot's motion.
Regarding open-loop control strategies, some studies \cite{khosravi1987control, leboeuf2012optimal} generated them by informing the optimization process with athletes' kinematic data.
In this case, it is not surprising that the optimal solutions matched athletes' kinematics.
However, these studies do not provide insights into why athletes use these acrobatic techniques, as the optimization did not aim to maximize performance.
Other predictive simulation studies \cite{pettersson2013optimisation, farr2025including} generated backward somersault techniques by informing only the task requirements (without providing athletes techniques).
In this case, the optimal techniques resembled athletes' techniques, but spotting and floor fixation did not appear in the optimal strategies because sensory feedback was not modelled.
A predictive simulation study focussing on twisting somersaults on the trampoline \cite{charbonneau2025including} generated spotting and floor fixation strategies by imposing these behaviors by incorporating them directly in the cost function.
However, this study does not provide insights into why athletes use these sensorimotor strategies, as the strategies were imposed rather than emerging from the optimization. 
To better match and thus better understand athletes' behaviour, further predictive simulations of the backward somersault are needed to include sensory feedback and motor variability.


The primary aim of this study was to produce a predictive simulation from which an optimal sensorimotor strategy for the backward somersault would emerge.
A torque-driven multi-body model governed by nonlinear dynamics under uncertainty was used.
\hl{To cope with the motor noise, the avatar could use noisy vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive information. This sensory information was used for direct and anticipatory feedback corrections.}
We hypothesized that 
\begin{itemize}
	\item[\textbf{H1}] allowing the avatar to use direct sensory feedback would enable reducing landing variability;
	\item[\textbf{H2}] allowing the avatar to also use anticipatory feedback would enable further reducing landing variability;
	\item[\textbf{H3}] considering state-dependent sensory noise would allow the emergence of sensory acuity strategies in the optimal technique;
	\item[\textbf{H4}] allowing the avatar to use direct and anticipatory sensory feedback, while considering state-dependent sensory noise and control-dependent motor noise, would generate techniques resembling those used by athletes.
\end{itemize}
To test these hypotheses, optimal acrobatic strategies were generated by solving a deterministic optimal control problem and four versions of a stochastic optimal control problem.
All of our hypotheses were confirmed.
Interestingly, our most advanced stochastic implementation enabled the emergence of the two sensory acquisition strategies used by athletes, confirming the relevance of the modelling approach for the generation of sensorimotor strategies during complex movements.

All implementations (see Sec.~\ref{sec:methods} for details) considered a rigid planar free-floating based athletic avatar composed of five segments (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}): trunk, head, arms, upper legs, and lower legs.
The digital athlete was torque-actuated and governed by the non-linear multibody dynamics equations.
\hl{The total torques were composed of :}
\begin{itemize}
	\item[\textbf{OCP}] \hl{open-loop joint torques}
	\item[\textbf{SOCP}] \hl{open-loop joint torques, direct feedbacks based on proprioceptive and vestibular information affected by random sensory noise of predefined magnitude, and random motor noise of predefined magnitude}
	\item[\textbf{SOCP}$\mathbf{_{\text{VN}}}$] \hl{open-loop joint torques, direct feedbacks based on proprioceptive and vestibular information affected by random sensory noise modulated by the head angular velocity, and random motor noise modulated by the joint torque actuation}
	\item[\textbf{SOCP}$\mathbf{^{\text{AF}}}$] \hl{open-loop joint torques, direct feedbacks based on proprioceptive and vestibular information affected by random sensory noise of predefined magnitude, anticipatory feedbacks based on vestibular and visual information affected by random sensory noise of predefined magnitude, and random motor noise of predefined magnitude}
	\item[\textbf{SOCP}$\mathbf{_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}}$] \hl{open-loop joint torques, direct feedbacks based on proprioceptive and vestibular information affected by random sensory noise modulated by the head angular velocity, anticipatory feedbacks based on vestibular and visual information affected by random sensory noise modulated by the head angular velocity and gaze orientation, and random motor noise modulated by the joint torque actuation.}
\end{itemize}
\hl{For the deterministic implementation (OCP), a single unperturbed technique was optimized.
Whereas for the stochastic implementations (SOCP, \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$}, \mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$}, \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}) multiple techniques affected by different noise values were optimized simultaneously to find a unique control strategy (open-loop joint torques and feedback gains) that optimizes the performance of all techniques.}
	
The optimal policies were computed using a composite cost function, where the main objective was to minimize landing condition variability \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_1$})}, measured as the sum of the variances (n=20) of the CoM horizontal position \hl{in \mbox{$m$}}, CoM horizontal velocity in \hl{\mbox{$\nicefrac{m}{s}$}}, and body angular velocity \hl{(computed as the angular momentum divided by the moment of inertia)} in \mbox{$\nicefrac{rad}{s}$} at the end of the movement.
Other terms minimized the expected efforts \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_2$}; joint torques in \mbox{$Nm$})} and the expected \hl{time derivative of} efforts \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_3$}; joint torques derivative in \mbox{$\nicefrac{Nm}{s}$})}.
Finally, regularization terms encouraged a realistic motion: minimizing the direct feedback gains \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_4$})}, the \hl{time derivative of the} direct feedback gains \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_5$})}, and the duration of the movement \hl{in \mbox{$s$}} to avoid landing in a crouched posture \hl{(\mbox{$\phi_6$})}.
\hl{For the deterministic implementation (OCP), the landing condition variability (\mbox{$\phi_1$}), feedback gains (\mbox{$\phi_4$}), and time derivative of the direct feedback gains (\mbox{$\phi_5$}) terms were set to zero in the cost function. However, to compare the robustness of this technique with the other implementations, the landing condition variability was computed post-optimization. Motor noise was randomly sampled and added to the optimal joint torques, and a series of forward simulations was performed.}

An optimal solution was reached for all implementations.
%, despite the large problem sizes (OCP: 303 variables, SOCP: 5625 variables, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$: 5625 variables, SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$: 6562 variables, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$: 6562 variables).
The optimization lasted a few seconds for the deterministic version (OCP), and a few weeks for the stochastic versions (SOCP, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$, SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$).
\hl{The optimal cost differed between implementation (OCP: 331.25, SOCP: 4928.34, \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$}: 106.26, \mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$}: 98.52, \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}: 87.70), with \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$} reaching the lowest cost function value.}

\subsection{Landing variability performance}

The main performance objective of the task considered in this study was to minimize landing variability.
All stochastic implementations outperformed the deterministic one in terms of landing variability (\hl{Fig.~\mbox{\ref{fig:landing_variability}}}; OCP: 14115.4, SOCP: 62.2, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$: 27.5, SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$: 21.2,
SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$: 21.2).
The smallest landing variability was achieved with the SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$ implementation \hl{(SD of about $9.82$ mm for the CoM position, $3.11$ mm/s for the CoM velocity, and $0.02 \degree$ for the body angular velocity).}
\hl{This improvement is mainly driven by the small variability of the body angular velocity, an improvement of more than two orders of magnitude compared to the OCP implementation.}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Optimal techniques were generated for the aerial phase (from take-off to landing) of the backward somersault using a \hl{deterministic implementation and four variations of stochastic implementations}. 
The landing conditions were compared across implementations to test our hypothesis that using direct sensory feedback would reduce landing variability (H1) and that using anticipatory sensory feedback would further reduce it (H2).
The first hypothesis was confirmed as the \hl{deterministic implementation}, the only \hl{one} without direct sensory feedback, had a landing variability larger by two orders of magnitude compared to stochastic implementations.
The second hypothesis was also confirmed as \hl{modeling anticipatory sensory feedback (\mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$ and SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}), allowed lowering landing variability}. 
\hl{The head stabilization strategy used by athletes (spotting) emerged from our implementations that considered state-dependent sensory noise (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$ and SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}), confirming our third hypothesis (H3).}
\hl{Our most advanced implementation (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}) generated techniques that more closely resembled those used by athletes, including gaze stabilization on the floor before landing, confirming our fourth hypothesis (H4).}
Interestingly, the technique generated with the deterministic (OCP) implementation resembled the technique used by athletes during their learning process, a period during which the technique is not mature yet.  


\subsection{Landing variability}

As observed in previous stochastic simulation studies \cite{van2022approximate, berret2024co}, including uncertainty in the optimal control problem implementation enabled the generation of optimal techniques more robust to motor noise. 
In line with our first hypothesis (H1), all our stochastic implementations (SOCP, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$, SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$, SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$) outperformed our deterministic implementation (OCP) in terms of landing variability, the main performance indicator in this study.
Furthermore, the \hl{smaller landing variability exhibited when including anticipatory feedback (\mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$ and SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}) confirms our second hypothesis (H2).
It shows that anticipatory feedback helps increase robustness to motor noise until landing.}
The beneficial role of anticipatory feedback (also called prospective regulation) during the backward somersault was also suggested by Bardy \& Laurent \cite{bardy1998body}.
They \hl{observed} that landing variability was increased in darkness (\textit{i.e.}, no vision) condition.   
The prospective regulation of landing variability \hl{in simulations with anticipatory feedback (\mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$ and SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$})} was mainly achieved by making adjustments to the shoulder joint kinematics (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} detail \circled{1}).
This strategy was also previously observed in trampolinists, where the upper-limb variability was increased during the last part of the acrobatics to allow reducing the body orientation variability at landing \cite{bourgeois2025motor}.
This upper-limb adjustment strategy offers an efficient way to regulate body rotation velocity by adjusting the moment of inertia, as these segments are less constrained by the acrobatic task ($g_2$: lower-limbs are constrained by the need to keep the center of mass above the feet).
In agreement with the measurement of athletes' variability regulation,  this study suggests that athletes use an internal model to predict their future state \hl{at landing} and make appropriate anticipatory corrections.


\subsection{Kinematics}

The optimal kinematics generated in this study generally reproduce athletes' key strategies with flexion and extension of the hips and knees, accompanied by arms lowering \cite{bradley2020comparison, leboeuf2012optimal}.
The kinematic differences between implementations are subtle, yet some of them are meaningful.
Interestingly, the \hl{deterministic (OCP)} optimal kinematics resembles the technique used by novice gymnasts and \hl{the most advanced stochastic simulation \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}} more experienced ones, although both implementations shared the same dynamical model and variable bounds (range of motion and joint torques).
This difference was noticeable in a looser tuck position in the \hl{deterministic (OCP)} compared to the \hl{most advanced stochastic (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$})} simulation, as observed in beginners compared to experts in \cite{kikuchi2006kinematical}.
This looser tuck position was caused by a smaller hip flexion and a later knee flexion (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} detail \circled{2}). 
In addition, a neck extension was observed during the elevation phase in the OCP kinematics (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} detail \circled{3}). 
This neck elevation is often noticeable in the technique of novice athletes as early as during the propulsion phase.
% Seul article où on voit ce lancé de tête, mais athlète charactérisé d'"élite" --' : IDENTIFICATION OF INTERNAL LOADS AT THE SELECTED JOINTS DURING PERFORMANCE OF A BACKWARD SOMERSAULT
In the current study, the propulsion phase was not simulated, leading to only a subtle neck extension.
In contrast, the \hl{most advanced stochastic (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$})} simulation presented neck flexion, stabilizing the head (see Sec.~\ref{sec:head_stabilization} for further discussion of head stabilization).
This discrepancy is also analogous to an athlete's behaviour modification through the learning process.
Natrup et al. \cite{natrup2021gaze} observed that less experienced athletes presented a neck extension and more experienced athletes did not during a backward somersault with a twist on the trampoline.
In continuation of previous findings \cite{harris1998signal, todorov2002optimal}, the present study suggests that athletes learn to adapt their motor strategy to account for their own motor variability.


\subsection{Head stabilization strategy}\label{sec:head_stabilization}
\hl{Our most advanced stochastic (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}) technique replicated the neck kinematics and head stabilization strategy used by athletes.
This suggests that our stochastic predictive simulation method is appropriate to allow the emergence of real-world sensory acquisition strategies.
Indeed, }Berthoz \& Pozzo \cite{berthoz1994head} highlighted that athletes exhibit two head stabilization phases: during the elevation phase after take off and in preparation for landing.
These two periods are characterized by a neck flexion compensating for the body's backward rotation.
Berthoz \& Pozzo \cite{berthoz1994head} interpreted that the first head stabilization allows the brain to use a combination of visual and vestibular cues to guide the movement and control the upcoming backward rotation velocity and acceleration.
Similarly, the second head stabilization would help landing coordination, using visual and vestibular information.
Between the two head stabilization periods, the neck is rapidly extended (referred to as head saccade).
This behaviour prevents the use of visual information but allows head repositioning to increase the duration of the period during which head stabilization is possible.

\hl{In the literature, the head stabilization periods are often thought to be mainly driven by the need for visual feedback \mbox{\cite{sanders1994head, luis2008visual, heinen2011evidence}}.
However, we observed that the stochastic implementation with only variable-dependent noise (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$}) and no visual feedback still presented this head stabilization technique.
Interestingly, our results suggest that athletes use head stabilization to enhance the acuity of vestibular feedback.}
\comEC{I had changed the order to put this last sentence first as asked by Friedl, but Mickael asked to change it back to the current order.}
In our simulations, head stabilization occurred when vestibular noise was modulated as a function of head angular velocity, which is in agreement with \mbox{\cite{davlin2004gymnasts}}.
They found that athletes still exhibited head stabilization periods in darkness conditions.
Thus, the current study strengthens, through a new numerical approach, the idea that head stabilization would allow increasing acuity of multiple sensory feedback, not only vision. 
\hl{Further research is needed validate the link between vestibular acuity and the head stabilization strategy.}


\subsection{Sensorimotor strategies}
Like in previous studies on reaching tasks \cite{van2022approximate, yeo2016optimal}, we obtained motor strategies composed of both open-loop control and feedback control (Fig.~\ref{fig:torques_contributions} b)).
Feedback use was modulated throughout the movement in our optimal solutions: direct feedback control mainly used during the middle part of the acrobatics and anticipatory feedback control mainly used during the second part.
A similar phenomenon was observed experimentally during the reaching task in a virtual reality setting, where feedback was modulated across the movement, with the feedback gains peaking in the middle of the movement \cite{dimitriou2013temporal}.
Our optimal techniques involve a more complex modulation of the feedback gains due to the increased task complexity.
The direct feedback gains were high for the shoulders at the beginning of the movement and for the hips during hip flexions ($\sim 25\%$ and $\sim 75\%$). 
These high gains indicate that these actions \hl{have the most impact on achieving} a controlled landing.
In addition, the knee's direct and anticipatory feedback gains were high throughout the movement.
Flexing/extending the knees and shoulders is a simple and effective way to modify the somersault velocity (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} detail \circled{4}), as the shanks and lower arms are far from the CoM and thus have a large impact on the body's moment of inertia.

In line with our fourth hypothesis (H4), \hl{the optimal technique of our most advanced stochastic implementation (\mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$})} presented a more pronounced gaze stabilization on the floor, reducing the uncertainty associated with the visual sensory information (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensory_strategies} b) and Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} detail \circled{4}).
\hl{Anticipatory feedback gains generally increased during periods of higher visual and vestibular acuity, the former provided by the gaze-on-floor strategy and the latter by the head stabilization strategy. This suggests that these kinematic features may have a sensory acquisition origin.}

\hl{However, a contradictory result emerged from the stochastic implementation with only anticipatory feedback (\mbox{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$}).
It exhibited a similar temporal evolution of anticipatory feedback gains as \mbox{SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$}, despite sensory acuity being modelled as independent of the state variables.
Thus, head stabilization and gaze-on-floor strategies have no effect on sensory noise in this condition. 
This unexpected result indicates that the anticipatory feedback gain temporal evolution may also have a biomechanical origin. 
Notably, high gains coincided with hip flexion moments, which must be precisely executed to achieve consistent landing.
Future research is required to validate or challenge this last hypothesis.}


\subsection{Limits and perspectives}\label{subsec:limits}
The modelling method proposed in this study better reproduced elite athletes' behaviour than a \hl{deterministic} optimal control implementation.
However, some limitations could still be addressed to further increase the realism of the optimal sensorimotor techniques generated:

\textit{i)} Grasping the shanks with the hands was neither imposed nor encouraged, although it is a behaviour observed in athletes and recommended by coached. \hl{To better match athletes' behaviour, } this could be added to the stochastic optimal control problem using holonomic tucking constraints, as in \cite{farr2025including}. However, it would largely increase the already long computational time.

\textit{ii)} As the first stochastic optimal control study applied to acrobatic movements, we only focused on the aerial phase of the acrobatics. However, future studies should also consider the propulsion and landing phases, as they are likely also largely affected by motor variability.

\textit{iii)} The number of episodes was chosen in agreement with \cite{koelewijn2020solution}, where it was shown that only 20 episodes were needed to approximate the stochastic optimal control simulation of gait accurately. Although the model rotating freely in space during the backward somersault induces greater nonlinearity in the system dynamics, reintegration with different noise values remained consistent with the optimal solutions \hl{(\mbox{Fig.~\ref{fig:reintegration} in Appendix~\ref{subsec:reintegration}})}.

\textit{iv)} Only one initial guess was tested for each implementation due to the large computational time needed to reach convergence (a few weeks per problem). Thus, \hl{different initial guesses might have yielded better local optima. However, to make sure to compare implementations fairly (near solution regions), all stochastic problems were initialized with the solution from the deterministic version of the problem.} Moreover, we observed a surprisingly low cost function value for the SOCP$_{\text{VN}}$, SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$, and SOCP$_{\text{VN}}^{\text{AF}}$ compared with the OCP implementation, considering that the OCP cost function does not include variability ($\phi_1$) and gain ($\phi_4$ and $\phi_5$) terms. Thus, we hypothesize that using 20 episodes simultaneously in the stochastic implementations has a similar effect as multi-starting the problem and allows to avoid some less optimal local minima. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.


\subsection{Conclusion}
We introduced, for the first time, an approximate stochastic optimal control implementation for simulating acrobatic movements including direct feedback, anticipatory feedback, state-dependent sensory noise and signal-dependent motor noise.
The optimal techniques replicated a key sensorimotor feature of athletes' technique: head stabilization after takeoff and before landing to increase sensory acuity. 
Being able to solve such a complex optimization problem opens promising avenues for optimizing intricate sensorimotor strategies during other human movements.



\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Athletic avatar}
The athletic avatar's generalized coordinates ($q$) comprised unactuated (forward translation, upward translation, and somersault rotation) and torque-actuated \hl{(neck, eyes, shoulders, hips, and knees)} degrees of freedom (DoFs).
All actuated joints but the eyes were dampened ($N_D =- 0.1~\dot{q}$).
The torque applied on each actuated DoF ($\tau_{\text{total}}$) was composed of different components depending on the implementation considered (see Sec.~\ref{subsec:OCP}-\ref{subsec:SOCP_plus}).
The  inertial characteristics were set to match those of a national male trampolinist determined using Yeadon's anthropometric model \cite{yeadon1990simulation2}.
The avatar was governed by the non-linear multibody dynamics equation:

\begin{equation}
F(q, \dot{q}) : 
	\begin{bmatrix}
	\ddot{q}^{\text{B}}  \\
	\ddot{q}^{\text{J}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	= M(q)^{-1} \left(
	{\begin{bmatrix}
	0_3 \\
	\tau_{\text{total}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	- N(q, \dot{q}) - N_D(\dot{q})} \right) \label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}

% 	\begin{bmatrix}
% 	0 \\
% 	0 \\
% 	0 \\
% 	0.1 \\
% 	0 \\
% 	0.1 \\
% 	0.1 \\
% 	0.1
% 	\end{bmatrix}

\noindent where $q$, $\dot{q}$ and $\ddot{q}$ are the generalized joint coordinates, velocities and accelerations, respectively. $\ddot{q}^{\text{B}}$ refers to the acceleration of the base segment (forward translation, upward translation, and somersault rotation) and $\ddot{q}^{\text{J}}$ to the joint (neck, eye, shoulder, hip, and knee) DoFs. $\tau_{\text{total}}$ are the torques applied on each joint, $M$ is the mass matrix, $N$ is the non-linear effect vector comprising Coriolis, centrifugal, and gravity effects, and $N_D$ is the joint damping.

\subsection{Discretized stochastic optimal control framework}\label{subsec:methods_DSOCP}

To transcribe the stochastic optimal control problems into a solvable deterministic optimal control problem, the motor and sensory noises were sampled from a Gaussian distribution at 20 different instances, as in \hl{\mbox{\cite{afschrift2018increased, koelewijn2020solution}}}.
\hl{In other words, we solved for a common motor plan (\mbox{$\textcolor{gray}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}$} and \mbox{$\textcolor{gray}{K}$}} \footnote{\hl{To simplify interpretation, each optimization variable is presented in colour.}}) \hl{controlling 20 executions of the task, each with different random motor and sensory noise values and thus different state values.}
The number of noise samples (referred to as episodes) was chosen based on the analysis in Koelewijn \& Van den Bogert \cite{koelewijn2020solution}.
They showed that the cost function value varied by less than 1\% when using 20 or more samples.The constant part of the motor and sensory noises ($w_{\circ}$) were sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a standard deviation of \hl{\mbox{$0.125~Nm\sqrt{s}$}} for the joint torque motor noise, \hl{\mbox{$\num{7.16e-4}\degree\sqrt{s}$}} for the joint angle proprioception, \hl{\mbox{$\num{6.45e-3}\degree/\sqrt{s}$}} for the joint velocity proprioception inputs, \hl{\mbox{$\num{7.16e-4}\degree\sqrt{s}$}} and \hl{\mbox{$\num{7.16e-4}\degree/\sqrt{s}$}} for the vestibular input, and \hl{\mbox{$\num{1.25e-5}~s^{\nicefrac{3}{2}}$}} for the visual inputs. 

The optimal policies were computed using a composite cost function to generate a realistic acrobatic movement, while ensuring numerical stability.
The main objective of the stochastic optimal control problems was to minimize landing variability\hl{ as athletes aim for a consistent performance.}
\hl{Here, we defined landing variability} as the variance in \hl{horizontal (y-axis)} center of mass position, \hl{horizontal} center of mass velocity, and \hl{somersault} body angular velocity at landing.
\begin{eqnarray}
\phi_1 = \num{1e+4}~ \quad \big( \sum_{n}^{episodes}{(CoM(\textcolor{gray}{q_n})_y -\overline{CoM(\textcolor{gray}{q})})_y^2} +\\
 \quad \sum_{n}^{episodes}{(\dot{CoM}(\textcolor{gray}{q_n})_y -\overline{\dot{CoM}(\textcolor{gray}{q})})_y^2} +\\
 \quad \sum_{n}^{episodes}{(L(\textcolor{gray}{q_n}, \textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}_n})/I(q_n) -\overline{L(\textcolor{gray}{q}, \textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}})/I(\textcolor{gray}{q})})^2} \big)
\end{eqnarray}
\noindent where $n$ is the number of the episode, $L$ is the whole body angular momentum, and $I$ is the whole body transverse moment of inertia.
An additional term was added to minimize the expected efforts needed to complete the acrobatics:
\begin{equation}
\phi_2 = 0.01~\int_{0}^{\textcolor{gray}{T}}{\tau_{\text{total}}^2~dt}
\end{equation}
Another term minimized the expected time derivative of efforts to promote a psychophysiological motor command:
\begin{equation}
\phi_3 = 0.01~\int_{0}^{\textcolor{gray}{T}}{{\dot{\tau}_{\text{total}}}^2~dt}
\end{equation}
Regularization terms were also added to ensure that the feedback gains, the time derivative of the feedback gains, and the total movement duration remained within acceptable ranges:
\begin{eqnarray}
\phi_4 = \num{1e-5}~\int_{0}^{\textcolor{gray}{T}}{\textcolor{gray}{K}^2~dt} \\
\phi_5 = 1~\int_{0}^{\textcolor{gray}{T}}{\dot{\textcolor{gray}{K}}^2~dt} \\
\phi_6 = 0.01~\textcolor{gray}{T}
\end{eqnarray}
The weights of the objective terms were chosen to achieve a solution that \hl{looked realistic enough}. % and \textit{ii)} had enough variability in the SOCP solution to leave room for improvement in the other implementations, but not so much as to compromise convergence.
The manual adjustment of the weights was limited to a few trial-and-errors due to the long computational time needed to achieve convergence.

Specific constraints were also added to ensure the athlete avatar landed the backward somersault on the floor ($g_1$) with its center of mass (CoM) above its forefeet ($g_2$).
%
\begin{eqnarray}
g_1 : P(\textcolor{gray}{q_i}, \textcolor{gray}{T})_z = 0 \\
g_2: P(\textcolor{gray}{q_i}, \textcolor{gray}{T})_y - CoM(\textcolor{gray}{q_i}, \textcolor{gray}{T})_y = 0
\end{eqnarray}
%
\noindent where $P$ is the position of the forefoot marker, $._y$ and $._z$ subscripts refer to the projection on the anteroposterior and vertical axes, respectively.
For all simulations, an initial standing posture with extended body and arms upward, vertical velocity ($2~m/s$) and somersault velocity ($450\degree/s$) were imposed to match real athletes' take-off conditions.
For the stochastic version, an additional constraint was added so that the reference ($\textcolor{gray}{\text{ref}}$) sensory input was equal to the mean sensory input ($S$) over all episodes.
\begin{equation}
g_3: \textcolor{gray}{\text{ref}_i} - \overline{S}(\textcolor{gray}{q_i}, \textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}_i}, t_i) = 0
\end{equation} 

It is worth noticing that the backward somersault is a highly constrained movement, limiting the number of viable techniques.
This movement was chosen because the sensorimotor strategies involved are well established \cite{bardy1998body, heinen2011evidence, davlin2004gymnasts, luis2008visual, natrup2021gaze}. 


The optimization problems were implemented in Bioptim \cite{michaud2022bioptim} \hl{in direct multiple-shooting with a five step \mbox{$4^{th}$} order Runge-Kutta integrator}, using the automatic differentiation provided by CasADi \cite{Andersson2019}, and solved using IPOPT \cite{wachter2006implementation} and the linear solver MA97 \cite{hogg2011hsl_ma97} with a tolerance of \num{1e-6}.
The total duration of the aerial phase of the backward somersault ($\textcolor{gray}{T}$) was included in the optimal control problem as an optimization variable.
\hl{The movement time was discretized into 16 equal-duration time intervals, over which the controls (\mbox{$\textcolor{gray}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}$} and \mbox{$K$}) were constant.}
Continuity constraints ($g_{\text{DMS}}$) were imposed at each time interval transitions to ensure continuous generalized coordinates and velocities in each of the 20 episodes:
%
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{DMS}} :  
\begin{bmatrix}
\textcolor{gray}{q_{j+1}} \\ 
\textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}_{i+1}}
\end{bmatrix} - \int_{t_i}^{t_{i+1}}{
\begin{bmatrix}
\textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}_i} \\ 
F(\textcolor{gray}{q_i}, \textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}_i}, \tau_{{\text{total}}_i})
\end{bmatrix}
dt} = 0
\end{equation}

\pagebreak
The complete approximate stochastic optimal control implementation was as follows:
\colorbox{yellow}{%
    \parbox{\linewidth}{%
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll@{}ll}
\underset{\textcolor{gray}{q}, \textcolor{gray}{\dot{q}}, \textcolor{gray}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}, \textcolor{gray}{\text{ref}}, \textcolor{gray}{K}, \textcolor{gray}{T}}{\min} & \phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3 + \phi_4 + \phi_5 + \phi_6 \\
\text{~~~~~~~~~s.t.} & g_{\text{DMS}}=0 \\

\subsubsection{Stochastic optimal control problem (\textcolor{SOCP_color}{SOCP})}\label{subsec:SOCP}
The basic stochastic implementation was composed of 20 episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:socp}). 
The torques applied to the joints were composed of the voluntary motor command ($\textcolor{SOCP_color}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}$), direct feedback corrections ($\tau_{\text{dfb}}$), and motor noise ($\tau_{\text{noise}}$).
The direct feedback corrections were based on proprioceptive (joint positions and velocities) and vestibular (body spatial orientation\hl{, defined as whole-body somersault angular momentum divided by whole-body moment of inertia}) information. 
The direct feedback corrections on joint torques ($\tau_{\text{dfb}}$ defined in Eq.~\figeq{fig:socp}{4}) were a linear combination of the differences between the current noisy sensory information and a sensory reference ($\textcolor{SOCP_color}{\text{ref}_{\text{dfb}}}$)\hl{, such that each joint torque received contributions from all sensory inputs.}
\hl{These direct feedbacks were weighted by a feedback gain matrix ($\textcolor{SOCP_color}{K_{\text{dfb}}}$), which was part of the optimization variables.} 

The sensory reference was constrained to represent the mean sensory information across all episodes.
The sensory information was affected by random noise (\hl{proprioceptive noise} $w_{\text{p}}$ and \hl{vestibular noise} $w_{\text{v}}$) sampled from a Gaussian distribution of constant magnitude.
\hl{For each episode, motor and sensory noises were directly sampled from a Gaussian distribution (\mbox{Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods_DSOCP}}).}

\subsubsection{Stochastic optimal control problem with anticipatory feedback (\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{SOCP$^{\text{AF}}$})}\label{subsec:SOCPA}
\hl{Independently of the first improvement}, our second improvement to the SOCP implementation was to consider anticipatory feedback corrections ($\tau_{\text{afb}}$).
The anticipatory feedback corrections were \hl{generated through a simple forward model to estimate the body orientation at landing from sensory information.}
\hl{The forward model consisted in an Euler integration of the body spatial orientation, based on} visual (time remaining until landing: $\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{T} - t_i$) and vestibular (body spatial orientation: $\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{{q_n}_\text{S}}$ and somersault velocity: $\dot{\theta}$) information\hl{: \mbox{$\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{{q_n}_{\text{S}}} + \dot{\theta}(\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{q_n}, \textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{\dot{q}_n}) \centerdot T_{\text{remaining}}(\textcolor{SOCP_FEEDFORWARD_color}{T})$}}.

The direct feedback corrections were identical to those in the SOCP implementation.
The sensory information was affected by random noise ($w_{\text{v/g}}$).
The random motor and sensory noise were directly sampled from a Gaussian distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:socp_feedforward}).

\subsection{Comparison of the optimal solutions}
The optimal kinematics generated with our five problem implementations were compared using statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D) \cite{pataky2012one}.
For the stochastic implementations, the groups used for the SPM1D analysis were composed of all 20 episodes, and for the deterministic implementation the single episode formed its own group.
\hl{For landing variability comparison, the solution from the OCP implementation was reintegrated 20 times using the same integrator (five steps \mbox{$4^{th}$} order Runge-Kutta) with the addition of motor noise on the joint torques.}
The optimal joint torques were split into their components to analyze the contribution of open-loop control ($\textcolor{gray}{\tau_{\text{voluntary}}}$), joint damping, direct feedback control ($\tau_{\text{dfb}}$), and anticipatory feedback control ($\tau_{\text{afb}}$).
The head angular velocity and gaze orientation were analyzed to extract sensory acquisition strategies from the optimal acrobatic techniques.

\subsection{\hl{Reintegration of the optimal solutions}}\label{subsec:reintegration}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Appendix}
\setcounter{figure}{0}
\renewcommand\thefigure{A.\arabic{figure}}

\hl{To confirm that 20 episodes were enough to capture the influence of motor and sensory noise on the optimal solution, the optimal techniques were reintegrated post-optimization.
The same integrator as during the optimization was used (5 steps \mbox{$4^{th}$} order Runge-Kutta).
A hundred reintegration were performed with different random noise values sampled from the same noise distribution as for the optimization.

We concluded that 20 episodes were enough to capture the stochasticity of our problem as the reintegrations showed similar patterns to the optimal techniques (\mbox{Fig.~\ref{fig:reintegration}}). 
Notably, the final state mean and distribution were almost the same between the optimal techniques (mean: black lines; optimal technique: dark lines on \mbox{Fig.~\ref{fig:reintegration}}) and the reintegrations (box plots on \mbox{Fig.~\ref{fig:reintegration}}).}
